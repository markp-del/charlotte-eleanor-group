The morning, worked backwards

A one o’clock
ceremony means a
ten-to-seven start.

Nobody tells brides this, and it is the single most useful thing on the page. A wedding morning is not built forwards from when you wake up. It is built backwards from the ceremony.

Fix the ceremony. Subtract the drive. Leave forty-five to sixty minutes clear at the end for the dress, the flowers, the parents and the photographs — that window is not spare time, it is the time weddings actually run late in. Then stack the heads backwards from there.

Here is a real one: bride and four of the party getting ready at a house in Mold, one o’clock ceremony at Highfield Hall in Northop, fifteen minutes up the road.

TimeWhat is happening
6:50I arrive and set up. Chair by the window, kit on a clear table, kettle optional but traditional.
7:05 – 9:45Four of the bridal party, about forty minutes each. Bridesmaids and mothers first, so nobody is sitting around finished for four hours.
9:45 – 11:15The bride. Ninety minutes, second-to-last, so the hair is fresh but the photographer has something to photograph when they arrive.
11:15 – 11:30Touch-ups and the veil. Held back deliberately — it is the shot everybody wants.
11:30 – 12:30Dress, flowers, parents, photographs. This hour is not slack; it is the hour weddings run late in.
12:30Cars leave for a one o'clock ceremony at Highfield Hall, fifteen minutes up the road.

Move that ceremony to half past eleven and the same morning starts at twenty past five. That is why the ceremony time is the first question, and why a second stylist — who takes two of the party in parallel — buys about eighty minutes back for everybody.

The trial

Worth it — but not
for the reason
you think.

Most stylists sell the trial on the look. The look is the smaller half of it.

The trial is how a stylist learns how your hair actually behaves — whether it holds a pin, how much padding it needs, how long it genuinely takes on your head rather than on an average head. That number is what the entire running order on the morning is built from. Without it, the timings are an estimate, and at weddings it is the timings that go wrong rather than the hair.

It is also the only place you find out that the picture does not translate. A style can be beautiful and still be wrong on you, and the commonest regret afterwards is not looking like yourself. You want to look like you, on a very good day.

And it is where the accessories get sorted — whether the veil anchors, whether the hairpiece sits, whether the extensions blend. Those are the three things that most often go wrong at eight in the morning on the day.

Practicalities

  • When — six weeks to four months before, and always after the dress and veil are decided. The neckline dictates the hair
  • How long — allow about two hours. There is room for two styles in one session before hair stops co-operating; a third is pushing it
  • Bring — printed photos, the veil or hairpiece, any extensions, and a picture of the dress
  • Come with — hair washed and blow-dried the night before. Clean, dry, no product, not straightened
  • Bring one person, not five. Past two opinions it becomes a committee and the bride stops being asked
  • Go out in it afterwards. Live in it for a few hours and see whether it holds, and whether you keep touching it
  • If you hate it, say so in the room — not in the car. A second trial is normal and not a failure

What is needed in the room

  • A dining chair by a window. Daylight beats any lamp, and a dining chair beats a sofa every single time — a low seat adds ten minutes to every head
  • A socket within reach of the chair, not across the room
  • A clear table for kit. The photographer is coming into this room
  • A mirror the bride can genuinely see herself in
  • Veil, hairpiece, extensions and photos out ready — not in a box upstairs
  • Skincare done, face clean before the start if make-up is being done too
  • Somebody to answer the door, and somewhere to park
  • Nobody touches the hair once it is finished. This is a rule, not a preference

Beyond weddings

Race days, proms
and the rest of it.

Hair up and occasion styling is £60 and is not only a wedding service. Chester runs two Ladies Days a year — one in May with the Boodles May Festival and one in August — plus a music weekend and an autumn festival, and the whole county dresses for them. A fascinator or a hat needs a style built to take a grip at a particular angle, which is the same skill as setting a veil and is worth mentioning at booking rather than producing on the day.

Prom season runs May to July and clusters right after the GCSEs. A group of five getting ready at one house is structurally the same job as a bridal party — same running order, same advantage in not having to get five teenagers to a salon on the same afternoon.

Then christenings, big birthdays, the Christmas party, and the evening out that wants something better than a blow-dry. All the same service.

When to get in touch

The wedding work peaks June to September. The enquiries peak January to May, and there is a reason for that: about one UK proposal in six happens between Christmas Eve and Valentine’s Day, and those couples start booking suppliers within weeks.

So a January enquiry is a busy enquiry even though January is the quietest month for actual weddings, and a March enquiry is as likely to be for next summer as for this one. Trials for a summer wedding land February to May.

Practically: if you are marrying on a summer Saturday, ask early. If you are marrying midweek in November, there is far more room than anybody will tell you.

Questions

Asked and
answered.

How much is wedding hair around Mold?

Here it is £120 for the bride on the morning, £60 for each member of the bridal party, and £60 for the trial. A bride plus four of the party is £295 as a package. Nationally the bride's hair runs £120 to £300 and party members £60 to £150, so these sit at the bottom of the national band and slightly above the local mobile average for the bride.

Does the trial cost extra on top of a booking fee?

No, and this is the part worth reading twice. Almost every stylist charges two things: a non-refundable booking fee to hold your date, typically around £100, and then the trial on top, typically £80 to £115. Here the £60 trial does both jobs — it books the trial and it holds your date. There is nothing else to pay until the morning.

How far ahead should I book?

Summer Saturdays go nine to eighteen months out, and that is the national norm rather than a sales line — August alone accounts for about one wedding in seven in England and Wales, and Saturdays take four ceremonies in ten. A quieter month or a midweek date can often be booked at a few weeks' notice.

Do I really need a trial?

Yes, and less for the look than for the morning. The trial is how a stylist finds out how your hair actually behaves and how long it genuinely takes on your head — which is what the entire running order is built from. Without one, the timings on the day are guesswork, and it is the timings that go wrong at weddings, not the hair.

When should the trial be?

Six weeks to four months before, and always after the dress and the veil are settled — the neckline dictates the hair more than anything else does. Any closer than six weeks and there is no room left to change your mind.

What do I bring to the trial?

Printed inspiration photos rather than a phone, the veil or hairpiece if you have it, any extensions you plan to wear, and a picture of the dress. Come with hair washed and blow-dried the night before — clean, dry, no product, and not straightened.

What if I do not like the trial?

Say so in the room. There is usually time for a second style in the same session, because hair will take about two before it stops co-operating. If you go away, think about it and change your mind, a second trial is completely normal and not a failure. What does not work is deciding on the morning.

Should I wash my hair the night before or on the day?

The night before, then blow-dry it properly. Day-old hair holds pins; freshly washed hair slides straight out of them. If your hair goes greasy overnight, wash it in the morning instead — but dry it completely. Damp hair kills the schedule.

How long does each person take on the morning?

Roughly 75 to 90 minutes for the bride and 35 to 45 minutes for each member of the party, plus about 15 minutes to set up on arrival and 20 minutes at the end for touch-ups and the veil.

What time will you arrive?

It is worked backwards, not forwards. Fix the ceremony time, subtract the drive to the venue, leave 45 to 60 minutes clear for the dress, the flowers, the cars and the photographs, then stack the heads back from there. A bride plus four for a one o'clock ceremony usually means arriving around ten to seven in the morning.

When do I need a second stylist?

The clean rule: if the running order means starting before seven in the morning, book a second pair of hands. One stylist handles a bride plus three comfortably for an afternoon ceremony. A bride plus five, or any ceremony before noon, and a second stylist stops being a luxury — it buys roughly eighty minutes back out of everyone's morning.

Do you stay until I leave?

Ask this of anyone you book, because not everyone does — some pack up the moment the last head is finished. Here the veil and the final touch-ups are the last thing done, deliberately, so the finish is there for the photographs rather than an hour old by the time the cars come.

Can I wear extensions?

Yes, and they often help with volume — but buy them, colour-match them and have them cut and blended before the trial, not the week before the wedding. Clip-ins are usually the right answer if you do not wear extensions normally. For fine or shorter hair, padding is often the cheaper fix and nobody can tell.

When does the veil go in?

Last, as one of the finishing touches. And buy it after the trial, not before, so it can be chosen to suit the style rather than the other way round. Lightweight veils anchor cleanly; heavy pieces drag a style down and hide it.

Will it survive a wet north Wales afternoon?

An updo with a proper foundation will take wind, rain, hugging and a full evening of dancing. Loose waves in drizzle are a different proposition — and that is exactly the sort of thing to decide at the trial rather than discover at four o'clock. You will be left with grips and hairspray either way.

Who actually does my hair on the morning?

Charlotte does. With a team-based supplier the person at your trial is not always the person on the day, which is worth asking. Here it is the same pair of hands throughout, and a second stylist is only added when the timings genuinely need one.

Do you travel to the venue or come to the house?

Either. Most brides get ready at home or at the venue, and both work — a dining chair by a window with a socket within reach is all that is needed. Venues around Mold and the villages are all inside the normal round: Soughton Hall and its Coach House, Highfield Hall, Northop Hall Country House, Beaufort Park, Pentrehobyn, Theatr Clwyd and the register office at Llwynegrin Hall.

Do you do prom and race-day hair?

Yes — hair up and occasion styling is £60. Chester holds two Ladies Days a year plus a music weekend and an autumn festival, and prom season runs May to July, so both get busy in the same way a wedding morning does. A fascinator or a hat needs a style built to take a grip at a particular angle, so mention it when you book rather than producing it on the day.

Should I tip?

No. It is genuinely appreciated and never expected — and a review or a recommendation is worth considerably more. The fifteen-to-twenty-per-cent figure that circulates on some UK sites is imported American etiquette and is not the norm here.
